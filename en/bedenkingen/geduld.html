What if results take years to show?

A vegetable garden promises something most other pursuits don't: you sow, and within a few weeks you harvest. That speed is addictive, and at the same time it hides an uncomfortable truth. What you harvest grows fast. What makes that harvest possible — a living, resilient soil — grows slowly. Very slowly. A soil structure that holds water, a fungal network that exchanges nutrients, a balanced population of soil organisms: you don't build that in one season, and often not in three.

That contrast creates a real problem, not just a feeling of impatience. You compare your own garden after one year of chaos gardening to a vegetable garden that's been dug over and fertilized every year for ten years, and the conclusion seems to be that it "isn't working." But you're comparing two different points on the same trajectory, as if a one-year-old tree had to prove it will ever bear fruit. The soil under your feet needs a history to do what you expect of it, and that history only starts counting from the moment you stop disturbing it.

What helps is not working harder but measuring differently. Instead of waiting for the end result — a soil that's "finished" — you can see the intermediate steps as proof that the system is building itself up: more worms under a shovelful of earth than last year, less compaction after a rain shower, weeds that change species because the soil itself is changing. Those aren't side notes — they're the only signals you get in real time from a process that itself takes years.

And sometimes the most honest attitude is simply this: accepting that you're partly gardening for whoever works this soil after you — yourself in five years, or someone else. That sounds less satisfying than a full harvest basket, but it's closer to how soils actually come into being. No forest became rich in one season. Why should a vegetable garden have to be any different?

Your harvest grows in weeks. The soil that makes it possible grows in years. Patience here isn't a virtue, it's simply the timeline the system runs on.
